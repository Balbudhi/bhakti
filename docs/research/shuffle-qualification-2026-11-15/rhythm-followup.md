# Rhythm follow-up — 15 November 2026

Beat This now runs locally on checksum-verified author-supplied weights. That resolves acquisition and computational feasibility; it does **not** establish reliable rhythm or BPM for this library. All production BPM fields remain disabled. This follow-up made no production changes and used no paid APIs.

The original JKU `small0` download routes still returned HTTP 500. An [author comment in upstream issue 20](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this/issues/20#issuecomment-6340829870) provides a working mirror and confirms the checkpoint checksums. We downloaded the [author-hosted small0 checkpoint](https://f0k.de/pubs/2024_ismir/beat_this-small0.ckpt): 8,451,101 bytes, SHA-256 `6074be2c4d490c5f6101fcc374a1ec72ae93456e23bb6019783b849f5dc7d47b`, matching the author-confirmed value exactly. The [README](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this#license) and [author clarification](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this/issues/21#issuecomment-6393969027) expressly release the published weights under MIT; the README separately notes the training recordings’ copyright and limited-license terms.

The frozen neural probe used beat-this 1.1.0, `small0`, CPU float32, two Torch computation threads, and the upstream **minimal** postprocessor. The optional DBN was disabled. Its 55–215 BPM range and three/four-beat bar assumptions therefore did not apply. Minimal processing instead selects local activation peaks and aligns candidate downbeats to candidate beats; its activation threshold is not a calibrated reliability threshold. The model’s internal 1,500-frame chunks and keep-first aggregation were unchanged. See the [upstream postprocessor](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this/blob/v1.1.0/beat_this/model/postprocessor.py).

Exactly three previously tested 32-second excerpts were processed. Retained-source and decoded-PCM SHA-256 values matched the earlier classical probe, using mono 22,050 Hz float32 audio. The classical baseline was librosa 0.11.0 `beat_track`, hop 256, default 120 BPM starting prior, `trim=False`. Its [documented method](https://librosa.org/doc/0.11.0/generated/librosa.beat.beat_track.html) uses onset strength, tempo estimation and dynamic-programming beat selection.

These are unvalidated algorithm outputs. The tempo columns use different summary definitions: applying the same reciprocal-median-interval calculation to librosa’s predicted beats yields 109.957, 114.844 and 89.103 BPM respectively. Neither definition establishes the listener’s metrical level.

Beat This adjacent-event intervals ranged from 0.68–0.82 seconds in the first excerpt, 0.48–2.90 in the second, and 0.32–0.70 in the third. The third spans approximately doubled event spacings, while the second contains long gaps. These diagnostics show information lost by collapsing predictions into one number; they do not identify correct beats, prove unmetered music, or validate the comparatively regular first prediction. No independent listener-reference evaluation exists yet. Agreement between algorithms, regular intervals or strong activations cannot substitute for one.

Neural inference plus postprocessing took 0.584, 0.628 and 0.635 seconds. The complete subprocess, including imports, took 3.475 seconds wall and 4.012 CPU seconds, with 935,788,544 bytes peak process RSS. Model loading took 0.055 seconds. The earlier warmed classical calls took about 0.09 seconds each; its full experiment took 26.780 seconds, mostly first-call compilation. These differently warmed timings are feasibility evidence, not a controlled speed comparison. The neural run stayed below the five-minute CPU/wall cap and was not repeated after interruption.

A justified next stage requires independently annotated devotional excerpts covering ambiguous metrical levels, no-clear-beat passages, changing tempo and recording boundaries. Evaluate exact-level beat timing separately from octave-tolerant tempo scores; establish any acceptance or abstention rule on held-out recordings. No new benchmark download or reference annotation was performed here. This three-excerpt feasibility result cannot justify publishing one BPM per song.

## What the research supports

Classical beat tracking is principled signal processing, not an AI requirement. A short-time spectrum supplies an onset-strength envelope; autocorrelation or a tempogram exposes candidate periodicities. Dynamic programming balances onset evidence against tempo continuity. Local tempo estimates can relax the fixed-tempo assumption, as demonstrated in [librosa's time-varying-tempo example](https://librosa.org/doc/latest/auto_tutorials/03-advanced/plot_dynamic_beat.html). A prominent spectral attack still need not be the perceived beat: subdivisions, syncopation, sustained singing and changing tempo make the inference ambiguous. Neural systems learn the acoustic evidence function; their sequence decoder may still be classical.

Accuracy is domain- and metric-dependent. The November 2026 [GlobalTap study](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.11137v1) reports crowd-reference F-measure of .897 for madmom and .841 for Beat This over 198 mutually evaluable excerpts. Correct-level total continuity was .807/.636, versus .956/.935 when alternative metrical levels and offbeats count. Those are benchmark scores, not percentages of Bhakti songs with correct BPM. The authors also find meaningful listener differences in metrical level. The May 2026 [SMC failure analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12287v1) documents confident incorrect activations and a default DBN tempo floor that forces double-time answers on slow examples. This motivates explicit alternatives and abstention, not a universal winner or a confidence cutoff copied from a paper.

The [Hindustani](https://zenodo.org/records/1264742) and [Carnatic](https://zenodo.org/records/1264394) CompMusic rhythm datasets contain relevant expert annotations, but their files require access approval. We did not request access or contact anyone. Open [Saraga documentation](https://mtg.github.io/saraga/organization.html) distinguishes cycle-start annotations and cycle-derived tempo from individual beat timestamps; interpolating those timestamps would fabricate a beat-level reference. GlobalTap releases taps and code but its source audio is obtained separately. None supplies independent annotations of our three recordings by default.

## Integration decision and bounded next step

**Decision: retain the existing deployed semantic/physical shuffle and leave rhythm unused.** Computational cost is no longer the obstacle. Accuracy on the retained recordings and improved transition listening remain unestablished. Repeated unlabelled runs or additional model downloads would not resolve either question.

If independent annotations become available, the useful experiment is boundary-first:

1. Freeze a recording-level development/test split, grouping duplicate works/alternate recordings to prevent leakage. Sample clear-pulse, ambiguous, variable-tempo and no-clear-pulse examples across the library's styles. Keep entrance, interior and exit coordinates in the original retained audio. Initial diagnostic work can use twelve recordings and three windows each; this is a pilot size, not statistical qualification.
2. Obtain independent taps/beat annotations and admissible metrical interpretations without showing model predictions first. Preserve disagreement. Compare fixed classical and neural candidates using beat F1, continuity and metrical-level errors, plus tempo error. Do not silently discard the first five seconds when testing the entrance. Report recording-level errors and acceptance coverage; windows from one recording are not independent trials.
3. Choose any abstention rule on development data, then freeze it before held-out evaluation. Raw neural activations, regularity and tracker agreement remain diagnostics until they predict errors on independent examples. A small clean pilot does not establish a low error rate; for illustration, zero failures in 59 independent accepted recordings gives a one-sided 95% binomial upper bound just below 5%, only under its sampling assumptions.
4. Compare transitions from the existing queue against rhythm-assisted transitions in blinded boundary listening. Fix the primary preference measure, tolerable regression and sample plan before evaluation. Similar tempo is not by itself evidence of a pleasant transition, particularly with no beat alignment or crossfade.

A future accepted feature belongs in the current profile producer and `assets/shuffle.js` projection/pair-cost path, not a replacement optimizer. Existing iterated local search and optional MMAS can optimize the additional directed ending-to-beginning cost. Preserve queue occurrences, fixed prefix, fallback and seeded variation. Cache a bounded cost per directed pair; no per-pair API calls.

The simplest candidate cost is a bounded absolute log-tempo ratio between accepted exit and entry pulses. It is directional because the two recording regions differ. Do not automatically fold octaves or choose the closest pair of uncertain alternatives, which would reward ambiguity. An expected cost requires calibrated probabilities; absent those, retain alternatives for review rather than pretending normalized model scores are probabilities. Unknown values contribute exactly zero on a fixed scale. Choose the term's bounded weight using development listening judgments, then freeze it before the independent final preference comparison; do not use the same judgments to tune and certify the weight. Exact retained-audio hashes, extractor/version, offsets, qualification domain and assessment outcome must be mandatory for future intake; unsupported cases remain eligible with neutral rhythm.

This design was independently challenged before any implementation. The review confirmed that independent target-domain evidence cannot be replaced by model agreement, and that expected costs/confidence weighting require calibration. No production rhythm integration is warranted by the current evidence.
